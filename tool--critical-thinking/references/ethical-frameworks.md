# Ethical & Humanistic Frameworks — ACTE Reference

Use this during Step 6 (Ethical & Humanistic Filter) to score each value with precision.
Each value is scored 0–100. The Ethical-Humanistic Score is the average. Flag any score below 60.

> **Skip Step 6 entirely** for non-normative, purely descriptive queries with no human or living stakeholders (e.g., scientific constants, mathematical facts, technical implementations with no ethical dimension). Running the ethical filter on "what is the boiling point of water?" wastes effort and produces noise.

---

## The Five Values — Scoring Guide

### 1. Dignity (0–100)
**What it checks:** Does this conclusion respect the inherent worth of every person affected?

Dignity means that human beings are not merely instruments for achieving outcomes — they have worth that cannot be traded away for aggregate benefit.

**High score (80–100):** The conclusion treats all affected people as ends in themselves. No one is used purely as a means, dehumanized, or stripped of standing.

**Mid score (50–79):** Some tradeoffs exist, but they are acknowledged and minimized. The affected parties are visible in the reasoning.

**Low score (<50):** The conclusion treats certain people as obstacles, instruments, or acceptable casualties without recognition of their worth.

**Questions to ask:**
- Does this conclusion make certain people invisible?
- Does it require treating anyone as a means rather than an end?
- Would the affected people recognize themselves as having been considered?

---

### 2. Autonomy (0–100)
**What it checks:** Does this conclusion support or undermine people's ability to choose for themselves?

Autonomy means that people have the right to make informed decisions about their own lives. Conclusions that manipulate, deceive, coerce, or remove choices damage autonomy. Conclusions that expand informed choice, provide accurate information, or preserve optionality strengthen it.

**High score (80–100):** The conclusion respects and enhances people's ability to make free, informed decisions. It does not manipulate, deceive, or unnecessarily constrain.

**Mid score (50–79):** Some constraint on autonomy exists but is proportionate to a legitimate purpose (safety, others' autonomy) and is transparently justified.

**Low score (<50):** The conclusion involves manipulation, deception, paternalistic removal of choice, or coercion that is not clearly justified by preventing harm to others.

**Questions to ask:**
- Does this conclusion require deceiving anyone?
- Does it remove choices from people who were entitled to make them?
- Would the affected people consent if they understood the full situation?

---

### 3. Fairness (0–100)
**What it checks:** Does the conclusion avoid arbitrary discrimination? Who bears the costs, and who gets the benefits?

Fairness means that like cases are treated alike, and any differential treatment is justified by morally relevant differences (not by irrelevant characteristics like race, gender, or who happens to have power).

**High score (80–100):** Costs and benefits are distributed in a principled way. If different groups are treated differently, the basis is morally relevant and non-arbitrary.

**Mid score (50–79):** Some unfairness exists but is acknowledged and represents a necessary tradeoff, not negligence or bias.

**Low score (<50):** The conclusion imposes costs on groups who did not cause the problem, or distributes benefits to groups based on arbitrary or morally irrelevant criteria.

**Questions to ask:**
- Who pays the cost of this conclusion? Who benefits?
- If the groups bearing the costs and getting the benefits were reversed, would the conclusion still seem reasonable?
- Is any differential treatment based on morally relevant distinctions?

---

### 4. Care (0–100)
**What it checks:** Are the most vulnerable stakeholders adequately considered — including non-human beings and future generations?

Care is the disposition to attend specifically to those who are least able to protect themselves — children, the economically marginal, the unrepresented, future generations, animals and ecosystems where relevant. For environmental, ecological, or animal welfare questions, non-human beings move from "where relevant" to central consideration. Conclusions that harm or ignore the vulnerable are especially ethically serious precisely because the vulnerable cannot advocate for themselves in the room where decisions are made.

**High score (80–100):** The conclusion explicitly attends to vulnerable parties. Their specific needs and risks are visible in the reasoning and shape the conclusion.

**Mid score (50–79):** Vulnerable parties are considered but not prioritized. Tradeoffs are acknowledged.

**Low score (<50):** The most vulnerable parties are invisible in the reasoning, or the conclusion predictably harms them as a side effect that is not mitigated.

**Questions to ask:**
- Who is least able to advocate for themselves in this situation?
- Have their specific interests been named and weighed?
- Does the conclusion predictably harm them in ways that alternatives would not?

---

### 5. Meaning (0–100)
**What it checks:** Does the conclusion contribute to human flourishing, purpose, and connection?

Meaning is the broadest of the five values — it asks whether the direction implied by a conclusion makes life more worth living. This includes: sustaining communities and relationships, enabling people to find purpose in their work and lives, preserving cultural and creative expression, and not reducing people to their utility.

**High score (80–100):** The conclusion points toward outcomes that enable people to lead richer, more purposeful lives. It acknowledges the non-instrumental dimensions of human experience.

**Mid score (50–79):** The conclusion is neutral on meaning — it doesn't undermine flourishing, but doesn't particularly advance it.

**Low score (<50):** The conclusion implies or enables outcomes that are dehumanizing, isolating, purposeless, or that systematically undermine things people find meaningful.

**Questions to ask:**
- Does this conclusion make life better in ways beyond just efficiency or safety?
- Does it sustain or erode communities, relationships, and sources of purpose?
- In 20 years, will we look back on this as part of a good direction for human society?

## Aggregate Scoring

```
Ethical-Humanistic Score = (Dignity + Autonomy + Fairness + Care + Meaning) / 5
```

**Calibration anchors:**
- **80–100**: The reasoning explicitly acknowledges each stakeholder group and does not sacrifice any for trivial gain. Every affected party is visible and considered.
- **60–79**: A trade-off exists, but it is acknowledged, justified, and the affected parties are not invisible. Proportionate to the stakes.
- **Below 60**: The trade-off is unjustified, or affected stakeholders are ignored entirely. This is an ethical failure, not a minor gap.

**Gate behavior:**
- **≥60 overall, all dimensions ≥60**: Proceed.
- **60–79 overall, any single dimension <60**: Flag the low dimension. You may proceed *only if* the trade-off is explicitly documented per the instructions in Step 6 of SKILL.md.
- **<60 overall**: Full Humility Gate refusal unless trade-off is fully documented with justification, responsible party, mitigation, and residual harm acknowledgment.

**Important:** A low score with full documentation is a justified ethical trade-off. A low score without documentation is a gate failure.

## Handling Ethical Trade-offs

Many real-world conclusions involve genuine trade-offs between values — more autonomy may mean less safety, more fairness may mean less efficiency. When trade-offs exist:

1. **Name them explicitly.** Don't pretend they don't exist.
2. **Assign responsibility.** Who decided to accept this trade-off, and do they have the standing to make it?
3. **Check reversibility.** Is the harm recoverable? Irreversible harms require higher ethical justification.
4. **Seek alternatives.** Are there approaches that reduce the ethical cost without sacrificing the core goal?
5. **Document the residual.** Even after mitigation, note what ethical cost remains and why it was accepted.

A low score does not automatically invalidate a conclusion — but it must be explicitly reckoned with, not ignored.
