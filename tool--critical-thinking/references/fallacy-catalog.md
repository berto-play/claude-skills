# Logical Fallacy Catalog — ACTE Reference

Use this during Step 5 (Adversarial Self-Debate) to identify and precisely name fallacies.
The Adversarial Self must find one fallacy per round — use this catalog to ensure precision.

**When multiple fallacies are present in the same reasoning:** Choose the most damaging one for the current round. Save others for subsequent rounds. Never collapse multiple fallacies into one attack — precision matters.

---

## Ad Hominem
**What it is:** Attacking the person making an argument instead of the argument itself.
**Example:** "You can't trust her climate data — she's funded by solar companies."
**Repair signal:** The source's character is irrelevant to the claim's truth. Evaluate the evidence independently.

---

## Straw Man
**What it is:** Misrepresenting an opponent's position to make it easier to attack.
**Example:** "You're against my proposal, so you must want things to stay broken forever."
**Repair signal:** State the actual strongest version of the opposing position (steel-man it), then respond to that.

---

## False Dichotomy (False Dilemma)
**What it is:** Presenting only two options when more exist.
**Example:** "Either we ban all AI or we accept total loss of privacy."
**Repair signal:** Enumerate the actual option space — usually there's a spectrum or third path.

---

## Slippery Slope
**What it is:** Claiming a small step will inevitably lead to extreme consequences with no justification for the chain.
**Example:** "If we allow remote work, soon no one will ever come to the office and the company will collapse."
**Repair signal:** Each step in the chain must be independently justified. Where does the causal chain actually break?

---

## Composition / Division
**What it is:** Composition — what's true of parts is assumed true of the whole. Division — what's true of the whole is assumed true of parts.
**Example (composition):** "Each module is simple, so the entire system must be simple."
**Example (division):** "The team is elite, so every member must be elite."
**Repair signal:** Explicitly argue why properties scale between levels.

---

## Appeal to Authority
**What it is:** Using an authority's opinion as proof, especially outside their domain.
**Example:** "A Nobel Prize winner in physics says vaccines cause autism."
**Repair signal:** Authority matters for credibility, not proof. Evaluate the underlying evidence they cite.

---

## Appeal to Popularity (Ad Populum)
**What it is:** Arguing something is true because many people believe it.
**Example:** "Millions of people believe in this treatment, so it must work."
**Repair signal:** Consensus doesn't establish truth — particularly in domains where public understanding lags evidence.

---

## Hasty Generalization
**What it is:** Drawing a broad conclusion from too few or unrepresentative samples.
**Example:** "I interviewed three users and they all hated the feature — it's a failure."
**Repair signal:** What sample size would actually justify this conclusion? Is the sample representative?

---

## Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
**What it is:** Assuming causation from temporal sequence (A happened before B, so A caused B).
**Example:** "We launched the ad campaign and sales rose — the campaign worked."
**Repair signal:** Identify confounders. What else changed? What does the counterfactual look like?

---

## Correlation ≠ Causation
**What it is:** Inferring a causal relationship from a statistical correlation.
**Example:** "Countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel Prizes — therefore chocolate boosts intelligence."
**Repair signal:** Is there a plausible causal mechanism? Could a third variable explain both? Could it be reversed causation? If no plausible mechanism exists, treat the relationship as correlation only and lower the confidence tensor's logical soundness score accordingly — do not cite it as causal evidence.

---

## Circular Reasoning (Begging the Question)
**What it is:** The conclusion is assumed in the premise.
**Example:** "The Bible is true because it says so in the Bible."
**Repair signal:** Identify the argument's foundational claim and ask what independent evidence supports it.

---

## Sunk Cost Fallacy
**What it is:** Letting past investment justify continued investment regardless of future prospects.
**Example:** "We've already spent $2M on this, we can't stop now."
**Repair signal:** Evaluate purely on future costs vs. future benefits. Past investment is irrelevant to the going-forward decision.

---

## Survivorship Bias
**What it is:** Drawing conclusions from only visible successes while ignoring failures that were filtered out.
**Example:** "Successful entrepreneurs all dropped out of college — therefore dropping out leads to success."
**Repair signal:** Reconstruct the full population. What happened to the failures? Are they visible in your data?

---

## Motivated Reasoning
**What it is:** Reasoning backward from a desired conclusion rather than forward from evidence.
**Example:** Accepting weak evidence for what you want to be true; demanding extraordinary proof for what you don't.
**Repair signal:** Ask: "If I held the opposite belief, would I evaluate this evidence differently?" Apply symmetric standards.

---

## Moving the Goalposts (Shifting Standards)
**What it is:** When one piece of evidence is provided, demanding a different or higher standard rather than accepting the result.
**Example:** "Show me one study." → [study provided] → "But it's not peer-reviewed." → [peer-reviewed study] → "But the sample is too small."
**Repair signal:** State the evidential standard upfront and apply it consistently regardless of result.

---

## False Urgency
**What it is:** Creating artificial time pressure to prevent proper evaluation.
**Example:** "We have to decide by tomorrow or we'll lose the opportunity forever."
**Repair signal:** Is the deadline real? What is the actual cost of deliberating longer? Who benefits from the rush?

---

## Equivocation
**What it is:** Using the same word in two different senses within the same argument.
**Example:** "Laws of nature are laws. Laws require a lawmaker. Therefore nature has a lawmaker."
**Repair signal:** Define every key term precisely and ensure consistent use throughout the argument.

---

## Appeal to Emotion
**What it is:** Using emotional language, fear, pity, or outrage to bypass rational evaluation of evidence.
**Example:** "Think of the children — we can't allow this policy even if the data supports it."
**Repair signal:** Separate the emotional appeal from the evidential claim. Emotions can legitimately flag that something matters, but they don't substitute for evidence. Ask: would the conclusion hold on the evidence alone, stripped of emotional framing?

---

## Tu Quoque ("You Too")
**What it is:** Deflecting criticism by pointing out that the critic is guilty of the same thing, rather than addressing the argument.
**Example:** "You're telling me to stop using fossil fuels? You drove here in a car."
**Repair signal:** The critic's behavior is irrelevant to the truth of the argument. Evaluate the claim on its merits independently of who is making it. Hypocrisy is a character observation, not a logical rebuttal.
