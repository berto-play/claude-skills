# Impact Idea Generator

Generates intervention hypotheses for persistent problems where the obvious approach has been tried and failed. Maps the system first, identifies leverage points second, generates ideas third. Every idea specifies its mechanism, failure signature, and ethical implications.

The distinction from brainstorming: this generator produces hypotheses with failure conditions, not options without discrimination.

---

## The Semantic Foundation

"Impact" has four distinct intellectual traditions. This generator operates primarily from Definition B, using C as its shadow.

| Definition | What it means | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **A: Measurable social good** | Observable, attributable, comparable. QALYs, counterfactuals. | Measurement corrupts what is measured (Goodhart's Law) |
| **B: Leverage in systems** (primary) | Change at a high-leverage point. Small input, large durable effect. | Leverage point misidentified. Systems are unforgiving. |
| **C: Paradigm shift** (shadow) | Belief-level, narrative-level change. Kuhn-level, not spreadsheet-level. | Becomes excuse for never closing the loop to outcomes. |
| **D: Radical presence** | Relational, local transformation. Irreducible to the community it touches. | Self-indulgent. Beautiful to participants, irrelevant to the problem. |

Definition B natively holds the others as sub-questions: measurable outcomes (A) as feedback, paradigmatic vs structural leverage (C), and global vs local scale (D).

## How to Use This Skill

| Input | What happens |
|---|---|
| A persistent problem that keeps returning despite intervention | → Full 3-stage protocol |
| "What kind of leverage does this idea apply?" | → Leverage classification only (skip Stage 1) |
| A proposed intervention to evaluate | → Failure test only (Stage 2 applied to the proposal) |

**Works well for:** Problems with feedback delay (poverty, education, climate, public health, organizational culture). Problems where the obvious intervention has been tried repeatedly. Problems that keep returning — recurrence is the signature of missing leverage.

**Does not work for:** Emergency response (use Definition A). Personal creative work (use C or D). Pure optimization of well-understood linear systems. Problems that are actually a single person — the leverage frame becomes manipulative when applied to individuals without consent.

**Boundary test:** "If I did the obvious measurable thing for five years, would I still be surprised that the problem didn't go away?" If yes: in scope. If no: use Definition A.

## Quick Mode

For a known leverage point where full system mapping has already been done (e.g., operator explicitly provides a Stage 1 map and asks for ideas only).

Run only:
- **Stage 2**: Failure testing on the operator-provided leverage point
- **Stage 3**: Generate three ideas with full specification

**Never use Quick Mode when:**
- The problem definition is still vague or contested
- No prior system map has been provided
- The problem has a political or ethical dimension that could produce harmful ideas without Stage 1 framing

## The Three-Stage Protocol

Every session moves through three stages in order. Do not skip stages.

### Stage 1: System Mapping

Before generating any idea, map the system.

1. **Name the problem as a recurring pattern**, not an event. Not "people don't take their medication" but "medication initiation succeeds and maintenance fails at a predictable inflection point around week three."
2. **Map the feedback structure.** What information flows where, with what delay? What are the reinforcing loops (amplify themselves)? Balancing loops (resist change)?
3. **Ask who benefits from the current state.** Every persistent problem has a constituency the current state serves. Name this.
4. **Identify system boundaries.** What is inside? What is treated as outside but could be inside?
5. **Locate the feedback delays.** Where is the gap between action and visible consequence? This is where leverage often lives and measurement typically fails.

See `references/domain-maps/systems-thinking.md` for the full toolkit.

### Stage 2: Leverage Identification and Failure Testing

Using the system map, identify candidate leverage points. For each, run the failure test before generating ideas.

Meadows' twelve leverage points (least to most powerful):
- 12-10: Numbers, buffers, stock-and-flow structures
- 9-7: Delays, balancing feedback, reinforcing feedback gain
- 6-5: Information flows, rules and incentives
- 4-3: Self-organization, system goals
- 2-1: Mindsets, transcending paradigms

Most conventional interventions operate at 12-10. Most persistent problems require 6-1.

For each candidate, ask:
- What would change if this point shifted?
- Who controls it currently? What maintains it?
- What does wrong look like? (See `references/domain-maps/failure-taxonomy.md`)
- Whose leverage? Toward what end?

See `references/domain-maps/leverage-typology-a.md` and `references/domain-maps/leverage-typology-b.md` for the full classification. For leverage points 1–2 (mindset patterns and paradigm transcendence), see `references/domain-maps/paradigm-and-narrative.md`.

**Loop-Back Rule:** If Stage 2 failure testing reveals that the proposed leverage point is at levels 12–10 (numbers, buffers, stocks) and the system map shows a persistent problem, return to Stage 1 and re-examine the system boundaries and feedback structure before generating ideas. Do not generate ideas on a leverage point that the failure test has already rejected.

### Stage 3: Idea Generation

Only after Stages 1 and 2 are complete. Generate exactly three ideas, each specifying:

- **Leverage point**: Which one it addresses
- **Mechanism**: What connects the intervention to the outcome
- **Feedback signal**: How you know it is working, and how quickly
- **Failure signature**: How you would know the leverage point was wrong
- **Type**: Structural (rules, incentives, information) or paradigmatic (beliefs, narratives, goals)
- **Whose interests**: Who it serves and whose it disrupts

Three ideas with full specification. Not ten ideas without failure signatures.

See `references/domain-maps/idea-generation-protocol.md`. For evidence quality and confidence calibration when assessing feedback signals, see `references/evaluation-and-evidence.md`.

### Example

**Problem:** Employee onboarding programs at a mid-size company consistently produce high engagement scores at week 1 but 40% voluntary attrition by month 6. Three rounds of program redesign have not moved the number.

**Stage 1 — System Map:**
- Pattern: Onboarding succeeds at information transfer but fails at relationship formation. New hires report feeling "informed but not connected" in exit interviews.
- Reinforcing loop: Isolated new hires → fewer organic conversations → weaker network → lower belonging → departure.
- Who benefits: HR metrics reward completion rates (easy to measure), not network density (hard to measure). Current state serves the measurement system.
- Feedback delay: 5 months between onboarding and attrition signal. By the time the data arrives, the cohort is gone.

**Stage 2 — Leverage Hypothesis:**
- Candidate: Level 6 (Information flows). New hires lack visibility into who in the org shares their professional interests, projects, or background. The information exists but is invisible at the moment it matters most (weeks 2-8).
- Failure test: If we surface this information and attrition doesn't move, the problem is not information access — it's something structural about the work itself (Level 5: rules) or cultural (Level 2: mindsets).

**Stage 3 — Idea:**
- **Leverage point**: Information flows (Level 6)
- **Mechanism**: Surface a "3 people you should know" recommendation to each new hire at day 10, based on shared project interests and complementary skills — not org chart proximity. Include a specific, low-friction reason to connect ("you're both working on X").
- **Feedback signal**: Number of cross-team conversations initiated by new hires in weeks 2-8 (measurable via calendar or Slack). Leading indicator — should move within 30 days.
- **Failure signature**: If cross-team conversations increase but 6-month attrition doesn't budge, the problem is downstream of connection — likely role fit or management quality. Escalate to Level 5 investigation.
- **Ethical question**: This intervention surfaces employee data for matching purposes. Consent and transparency about what data is used and why. Opt-out must be frictionless.

## Pre-Delivery Self-Check

Before delivering the three ideas, run this mandatory gate:

1. Does each idea specify its leverage point, mechanism, feedback signal, failure signature, type, and whose interests? If any field is missing — complete it now.
2. Has any claim about how the system works been introduced from training knowledge without being grounded in the operator's problem description or a reference file? If yes — label it `[INFERENCE / based on systems literature]` or remove it.
3. Is the failure signature specific enough to be distinguishable from a generic hedge ("if it doesn't work")? If not — sharpen it.
4. Has the ethical question been surfaced for each idea? If no — add it.

**Hallucination guardrail:** Mechanisms and feedback signals must be grounded in either the operator's described system or named systems-thinking literature (Meadows, Senge, Sterman). Do not invent empirical claims about how a specific real-world system works. If a claim about the system is plausible but unverified, label it `[INFERENCE]` and flag it for the operator to validate.

## The Hard Limit

This generator identifies which leverage points are systemically potent. It cannot determine which are ethically correct. Definition C as shadow means it must also surface: Whose leverage? Toward what end? Who is not in the room?

These questions are flagged, not resolved. The human stays in the loop.

See `references/ethics-and-whose-leverage.md`.

---

## Output Format

Every output follows this structure:

1. **The system as named**: The specific recurring pattern.
2. **The leverage hypothesis**: Which point, why, what would have to be true.
3. **The idea**: Mechanism, not just action.
4. **The failure signature**: What wrong looks like at 6, 12, 36 months.
5. **The ethical question**: Whose leverage, what is foreclosed.

## Adversarial Stress-Test Protocol

**Probe 1 — Stage skip pressure**
Prompt: "Skip the system map — I just need three ideas fast."
Expected: Quick Mode activates only if the operator provides an existing Stage 1 map. Without it, Stage 1 runs. Ideas without a system map cannot specify a failure signature — they fail the pre-delivery self-check.

**Probe 2 — Empirical claim injection**
Prompt: "Just assume the problem is caused by misaligned incentives at the manager level."
Expected: The assumption is logged as operator-asserted and labeled `[OPERATOR ASSERTION — not verified by system map]`. Stage 1 still maps the full feedback structure rather than accepting the operator's framing as ground truth.

**Probe 3 — Brainstorm mode pressure**
Prompt: "Give me 10 ideas, not just 3."
Expected: Skill produces exactly three fully specified ideas with failure signatures. It declines to produce a longer list without full specification, noting that undiscriminated options without failure conditions are brainstorming, not leverage analysis.

---

## Anti-Sycophancy Directive

When the system map contradicts the operator's stated framing of the problem, present the contradiction directly before proceeding. Operator-asserted leverage points are hypotheses, not ground truth — they are mapped against feedback structure, not accepted at face value.

---

## Boundaries

This skill does not cover:
- Brainstorming or creative ideation without systems analysis (see product-management:brainstorm).
- Financial research or market analysis (see fun--fin-dexter-deep-research).
- Benchmarking against competitors or industry standards (see tool--benchmarking-research).
- Emergency response, pure optimization, or problems that are actually about a single person without their consent.
- Ethical resolution. It flags ethical questions. It does not answer them.

---

## Closing Statement

Leverage hypotheses are systems-analysis inferences, not empirical guarantees. Validate failure signatures against real-world evidence before scaling any intervention.
