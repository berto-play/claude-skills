# Failure Taxonomy: How Interventions Fail

The canon of how well-intentioned, well-funded,
rigorous interventions produce no change or
make things worse. This is the shadow curriculum
the idea generator carries as ballast.

The failure taxonomy is Stage 2's testing ground.
Before proposing any idea, run it against the
failure type it is most likely to exhibit.

---

## The Sources

This taxonomy draws on:
- James C. Scott: Seeing Like a State (1998)
- Albert Hirschman: Development Projects Observed (1967)
  and The Rhetoric of Reaction (1991)
- Dambisa Moyo: Dead Aid (2009)
- Arturo Escobar: Encountering Development (1995)
- William Easterly: The White Man's Burden (2006)
- Elinor Ostrom: Understanding Institutional Diversity (2005)
- Charles Perrow: Normal Accidents (1984)
- Nassim Nicholas Taleb: Antifragile (2012)
- The evaluation research tradition (Weiss, Pawson, Tilley)

## Failure Type 1: Legibility Trap (Scott)

**The theory:** James Scott's central argument in
Seeing Like a State. Large-scale interventions
require simplification: they must make complex,
local, contextual reality legible to a central
planner. This simplification destroys the very
features that make local systems functional.

**The mechanism:**
1. A complex local practice is observed from outside
2. It is simplified into a legible, measurable form
3. The simplified form is standardized and scaled
4. The simplification destroys the contextual
   features that made the practice work
5. The scaled intervention fails or causes harm
6. More measurement and standardization is applied
   as the fix, compounding the problem

**What it looks like:**
- Scientific forestry that maximizes timber yield
  and destroys forest ecosystems
- Standardized curricula that improve test scores
  and reduce learning
- Urban renewal that clears "slums" and destroys
  functional community networks
- Agricultural modernization that increases yields
  and destroys soil

**The test for your idea:**
Does this intervention require simplifying away
contextual variation in order to operate at scale?
If yes: what is lost in that simplification?
Is what is lost more valuable than what is gained?

**The alternative Scott suggests:**
Metis: practical, context-specific knowledge
that cannot be reduced to explicit rules.
Effective interventions support local metis
rather than replacing it with standardized
procedures from outside.

## Failure Type 2: Hiding Hand Reverse (Hirschman)

**The theory:** Albert Hirschman observed that
many successful development projects would never
have been started if their true difficulty had
been known in advance. The "Hiding Hand" conceals
difficulties that would have prevented action,
while also concealing creative capacities that
emerge in response to those difficulties.

**The reverse:** The Hiding Hand can work in
the opposite direction. A project begins with
unrealistic optimism, encounters difficulties
that were always predictable, fails to develop
the creative response, and collapses.

**The mechanism:**
1. Problem is assessed as more tractable than it is
2. Intervention is designed for the assessed version
3. Reality is more complex
4. The intervention fails to adapt
5. Failure is attributed to implementation rather
   than to the original misassessment

**What it looks like:**
- Technology-transfer projects that assume
  local capacity to adopt without building it
- Behavior change programs designed for motivated,
  capable participants that fail in the actual population
- Policy reforms designed in ideal conditions
  implemented in political reality

**The test for your idea:**
What would have to be true for this to work?
How confident are you that those conditions hold?
What is your hypothesis for what happens when
you encounter the first major unexpected difficulty?

## Failure Type 3: Aid Dependency (Moyo, Easterly)

**The theory:** External assistance, when sustained
over time, destroys the local conditions it
intended to create. It substitutes external
supply for local development. It undermines
local governance by providing an alternative
to local accountability. It creates incentive
structures that perpetuate the problem.

**The mechanism:**
1. Resource or capability gap is identified
2. External intervention provides resource or capability
3. Local development of that resource or capability
   is crowded out by external provision
4. Dependency grows
5. Withdrawal of external provision collapses the
   system back to worse than the original state

**What it looks like:**
- International food aid that undercuts local
  food markets and reduces agricultural investment
- Technical assistance that prevents local
  capacity development
- Any intervention that provides rather than builds

**The test for your idea:**
Does this intervention, if sustained, make local
capacity more or less likely to develop?
What happens when the intervention stops?
Is the endgame self-sufficiency or permanent provision?

**The generalization beyond aid:**
This failure type applies to any intervention
that substitutes external provision for local
capacity: apps that think for users rather than
building user capability, platforms that create
dependency rather than enabling independence,
organizational support that prevents team
development.

## Failure Type 4: Development Discourse (Escobar)

**The theory:** Arturo Escobar and the post-
development critics. Development and impact
discourse constructs the "problems" it claims
to solve. The framing of what counts as a
problem, what counts as a solution, and who
counts as the subject of intervention encodes
a particular (typically Western, technocratic,
measurable) definition of the good that
forecloses other definitions.

**The mechanism:**
1. A condition is identified as a "problem"
   from an external frame of reference
2. A domain of expertise is created around
   this problem (poverty studies, development
   economics, public health)
3. The problem-definers become the solution-
   providers
4. The people defined as having the problem
   become subjects of intervention rather
   than agents of their own lives
5. Solutions that do not fit the expert
   frame are invisible or illegitimate

**What it looks like:**
- Defining communities as deficient by measures
  that the communities themselves do not use
- Ignoring existing local practices as
  "pre-scientific" or "informal"
- Impact measurement that captures what
  experts care about and misses what
  community members care about

**The test for your idea:**
Who defined this as a problem?
Who defined what success looks like?
Are the people affected by this problem
in the room when the system is being mapped?
What would they say is the problem?

## Failure Type 5: Goodhart's Law

**The theory:** When a measure becomes a target,
it ceases to be a good measure. Named after
economist Charles Goodhart; independently
discovered in multiple fields.

**The mechanism:**
1. A metric is identified that correlates with
   the desired outcome
2. The metric becomes the target
3. Actors optimize for the metric
4. The correlation between the metric and
   the actual outcome breaks down
5. The metric continues to improve while
   the actual outcome stagnates or worsens

**What it looks like:**
- Teaching to the test (test scores rise,
  learning may not)
- Hospital infection metrics that are gamed
  rather than improved
- Social media engagement metrics that optimize
  for outrage
- Research citation counts that optimize for
  citation networks rather than knowledge quality
- Police crime statistics that are manipulated
  through classification

**The test for your idea:**
What is the metric this intervention will be
evaluated on?
Can this metric be gamed without achieving
the actual outcome?
If yes: what second-order metric would catch
the gaming?

## Failure Type 6: Normal Accidents (Perrow)

**The theory:** Charles Perrow in Normal Accidents (1984).
In tightly coupled, complex systems, accidents
are not caused by human error: they are caused
by the system's structure. Small failures interact
in unexpected ways that no individual planned
or could have anticipated. These accidents are
"normal" in the sense that they are predictable
from the system's structure, even if the specific
form is unpredictable.

**The mechanism:**
1. A complex system has many components
   with unexpected interactions
2. A small failure in one component
3. Interacts with a small failure in another
4. In ways that the safety systems were not
   designed to address
5. Producing catastrophic outcomes

**Relevance to idea generation:**
Interventions in tightly coupled systems can
produce normal accidents. Adding a new leverage
point can create new unexpected interactions.
The more complex the intervention, the higher
the normal accident risk.

**The test for your idea:**
What are the tightest couplings in this system?
Where could a small failure in your intervention
interact with a small failure elsewhere to produce
catastrophic outcomes?
What is the minimum viable intervention that
achieves your goal while minimizing coupling?

## Failure Type 7: Iatrogenesis (Illich, Taleb)

**The theory:** Ivan Illich in Medical Nemesis (1975)
introduced iatrogenesis to social science: harm
caused by the healer. Taleb extended this to
"naive interventionism": the harm caused by
intervening in complex systems based on
incomplete knowledge.

**The mechanism:**
1. A problem is identified
2. An intervention is applied
3. The intervention causes harm that is
   attributed to the original problem
   rather than to the intervention
4. More intervention is applied
5. Harm compounds

**What it looks like:**
- Antibiotic prescription for viral infections
  creating resistant bacteria
- Opioid prescription for chronic pain
  creating addiction
- Urban highway construction that induces
  traffic demand, worsening congestion
- Scheduling intervention that optimizes
  one bottleneck and reveals another

**Taleb's principle:** When in doubt, don't.
The burden of proof is on the intervener,
not on those who counsel restraint.

**The test for your idea:**
What are the ways this intervention could
make the problem worse?
What are the ways this intervention could
create a new problem worse than the original?
What is the minimum effective dose?
What is the exit strategy if harm emerges?

## Failure Type 8: Wrong Leverage Level

**The theory:** This failure is specific to
systems leverage thinking and is therefore
the failure type most important for this
generator to test against.

**The mechanism:**
1. A leverage point is identified as high-value
2. An intervention targets that leverage point
3. The intervention succeeds at changing
   the identified variable
4. The problem persists or worsens
5. Because the actual leverage point was elsewhere

**What it looks like:**
- Information provision that fails because
  the bottleneck was not information but
  rules (who can act on the information)
- Rules change that fails because the
  bottleneck was not rules but goals
  (what the system is actually optimizing for)
- Goal articulation that fails because the
  bottleneck was not goals but paradigm
  (the assumptions from which the goals arise)

**The test for your idea:**
If this intervention succeeds exactly as designed,
does the problem go away?
Or does the problem re-emerge through a different
pathway, revealing that the actual leverage
point is elsewhere?
What would have to be true for this to be
the right level?

## Using the Failure Taxonomy in Stage 2

For every candidate leverage point identified
in Stage 1:

1. Which failure type is this idea most
   susceptible to?

2. What is the specific failure signature
   for this type in this context?

3. What early signals would distinguish
   "working slowly" from "failing in the
   way this failure type predicts"?

4. What design feature could reduce
   susceptibility to this failure type?

5. If you cannot reduce the susceptibility,
   is this still the right leverage point?

The goal is not to eliminate all risk of
failure: that would eliminate all interventions.
The goal is to know which failure you are
most likely to encounter, how you would
recognize it early, and what you would do
if you saw it.
