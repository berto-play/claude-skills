# Leverage Typology — Part B: High-Leverage Points (Levels 6-1)

Continued from leverage-typology-a.md (Levels 12-7).

---

## Level 6: Information Flows

**What it is:** Who has access to what
information and when. Not the data itself
but the structure of who sees what.

**Why Meadows calls this powerful:**
"The most stunning systems changes come
from the redesign of information flows."
Missing or delayed information is the
most common cause of system malfunction.
Adding a new information flow where there
was none is often cheaper and more durable
than changing any structural parameter.

**Intervention types:**
- Make invisible information visible
  (energy dashboards, calorie labels,
  financial transparency)
- Route information to the decision-maker
  who can act on it, at the moment of decision
- Remove information asymmetries that
  benefit one actor at another's expense
- Create information flows that close
  feedback loops that are currently open

**Failure mode:** Providing information
that the recipient cannot act on, does not
have the capability to interpret, or that
arrives too late to change the decision.
"Information provision" as a substitute
for structural change.

**Examples:**
- Hospital-acquired infection rates fell
  dramatically when hand hygiene compliance
  was made visible in real time to staff
- Energy use dropped when households
  could see their consumption relative
  to neighbors (Opower/Oracle Utilities)
- Credit scores made invisible creditworthiness
  visible and restructured lending markets

## Level 5: Rules

**What it is:** The incentives, constraints,
and boundaries of the system. Laws.
Regulations. Contracts. Norms. The rules
that determine what is rewarded, what is
permitted, and what is prohibited.

**Why this is powerful:** Rules define
who can do what. Changing rules changes
what is possible without changing anyone's
preferences or capabilities.

**Intervention types:**
- Change who has access to the system
  (inclusion rules)
- Change what is rewarded (incentive structures)
- Change what is prohibited (regulation)
- Change who sets the rules (governance)

**Failure mode:** Rules that are not enforced.
Rules that are enforced on some actors but
not others. Rules designed by those who
benefit from the current rule set.

**The political problem:** Rules are the
leverage point most tightly controlled
by incumbents. Changing rules requires
either power within the rule-setting system
or power to change who sets the rules.

**Examples:**
- Seatbelt laws changed behavior more
  effectively than seatbelt education campaigns
- Fiduciary duty rules changed financial
  advisor behavior more than ethics training
- Zoning rules determine what cities
  can build more than design guidelines

## Level 4: Self-Organization

**What it is:** The system's ability to
change its own structure. To evolve new
feedback loops, new stocks, new rules.
Biological evolution. Cultural learning.
Organizational adaptation.

**Why this is powerful:** A system that
can reorganize itself is more resilient
than any fixed structure. It can generate
solutions that no designer anticipated.

**Intervention:** Protect and nurture
the system's capacity for self-organization.
Create conditions for diversity,
experimentation, and the selection of
what works. Remove the conditions that
prevent self-organization (monopoly control,
suppression of feedback, enforced uniformity).

**Failure mode:** Optimizing self-organizing
systems for efficiency. Reducing diversity
in the name of consistency. Suppressing
local variation in the name of scale.
These interventions destroy the system's
capacity for adaptation precisely when
it will need it most.

**Examples:**
- Monoculture agriculture is highly efficient
  and catastrophically fragile
- Centralized educational systems that
  standardize away local adaptation
- Organizations that suppress dissent
  and lose the capacity to learn

## Level 3: Goals

**What it is:** The purpose or function
of the system. What the system is trying
to achieve. Not what anyone says the goal
is: what the system actually optimizes for,
revealed by its behavior over time.

**Why this is near the top:** Change the
goal of a system and everything else
changes. Rules, information flows,
feedback loops: all will realign toward
the new goal. Or reveal that the new
stated goal and the actual system goal
are different, which is itself important
information.

**The diagnostic question:**
"What would this system have to be
optimizing for in order to produce
the behavior we observe?"
This question reveals the actual goal
when the stated goal and the behavior
are inconsistent.

**Failure mode:** Changing stated goals
without changing the incentive structures
(rules) and feedback loops that maintain
the actual goal. Mission statements
that contradict the incentive system.

**Examples:**
- Healthcare systems that state the goal
  of health but optimize for volume of
  procedures: changing the billing code
  (a rules change) may be necessary but
  not sufficient if the underlying goal
  remains volume
- Education systems that state the goal
  of learning but optimize for test scores

## Level 2: Mindsets and Paradigms

**What it is:** The shared assumptions,
values, and worldviews out of which
the system arises. The paradigm from
which goals, rules, information structures,
and feedback loops emerge.

**Why this is the second most powerful lever:**
Paradigm shifts change everything downstream.
When enough people in a society change
their core beliefs, goals change, rules
change, and then information flows and
parameters adjust. The shift from
"poverty is a moral failure" to "poverty
is a structural outcome" changed what
interventions were considered appropriate.

**How paradigms change:**
Kuhn: anomalies accumulate until the
old paradigm cannot accommodate them.
A new paradigm emerges not by refuting
the old one but by offering a framework
that makes the anomalies coherent.

**Intervention types:**
- Surface the current paradigm explicitly
  (most paradigms are invisible to those
  inside them)
- Demonstrate anomalies that the current
  paradigm cannot explain
- Articulate an alternative paradigm that
  makes the anomalies coherent
- Create conditions for the new paradigm
  to spread (education, media, narrative,
  lived experience)

**Failure mode:** Paradigm-shift language
used to avoid accountability for material
outcomes. "We're shifting the paradigm"
as a substitute for evidence that anything
has changed.

This is why Definition C (paradigm shift)
is the shadow and not the primary.
C without B's discipline produces
beautiful provocation with no traction.

## Level 1: Transcending Paradigms

**What it is:** The capacity to hold
any paradigm lightly. To step outside
the current worldview entirely and
choose a different one. To recognize
that all paradigms are constructed
and therefore changeable.

**Why this is the highest leverage:**
"If no paradigm is right, you can choose
whatever one will help to achieve your
purpose." (Meadows)

**Who operates here:**
Artists, mystics, revolutionaries,
scientists at paradigm-breaking moments.
Not because they are special but because
they have practiced the capacity for
radical reframing.

**What this means for the idea generator:**
At this level, the generator asks not
"how do we fix this system" but "what
if the system itself is the problem?"
What if the frame that makes this problem
visible also makes certain solutions
invisible? What would an actor without
this frame see?

**Failure mode:** Using level 1 as a
reason to never commit. Endless
reframing without action. The leap
from "all paradigms are constructed"
to "therefore nothing can be changed"
is not logic: it is a choice made by
someone who benefits from paralysis.

## Reading the Twelve Levels Together

**The pattern of resistant change:**
Persistent problems are typically maintained
by leverage points at levels 5-2 that
are held in place by actors who benefit
from the current state. They are addressed
by interventions at levels 12-9 that are
safe, measurable, and ineffective.

**The idea generator's job:**
Identify which level 6, 5, 4, 3, or 2
intervention has been avoided and why.
Generate ideas that address that level.
Test those ideas against the failure
taxonomy before proposing them.

**The ethical question at every level:**
Who controls this leverage point?
Who benefits from keeping it fixed?
Who would be harmed if it changed?
And who was not asked?
