# Idea Generation Protocol

The operational process. How the generator
actually runs from problem statement to
fully specified idea.

This is not a brainstorming guide. It is a
hypothesis generation discipline.

---

## Before Stage 1: The Intake Questions

Ask before mapping anything.

**1. What is the recurring pattern?**
Not the event. The pattern. If the answer
is a single event ("last quarter our
user retention dropped"), push:
"How long has this been happening?
What does the data look like over
two years? Three?"

If this is genuinely a novel event,
the generator may not be the right tool.
Recurrence is the signature of a
systems problem.

**2. What has already been tried?**
List every conventional intervention
attempted in the last five years.
For each one: what was the result?
Not what was hoped. What was observed.

This serves two purposes:
- Identifies the leverage level of
  failed interventions (almost always
  levels 12-9)
- Builds the evidence that higher-level
  leverage is required

**3. Who benefits from the current state?**
This is the question most stakeholders
resist. Every persistent problem has a
constituency. Name it before mapping
the system.

**4. Who is not in the room?**
Whose experience of the problem is missing
from the current framing? What would they
say is the problem? What would they say
is the solution?

**5. What does the boundary condition test say?**
"If I did the obvious measurable thing
for five years, would I still be surprised
that the problem didn't go away?"

If the answer is no, this is a Level 12
problem and the generator is the wrong tool.
Recommend Definition A approaches.

## Stage 1: System Mapping (Detailed)

### Step 1: Name the Problem as a Dynamic

Translate the problem statement from a
snapshot to a time series.

Instead of: "Low medication adherence"
Ask: "When does adherence fail? At initiation,
at week 3, at month 6? Is the failure
rate constant or does it have a pattern?
What triggers the failure events?"

A problem named as a dynamic reveals
the feedback structure. A problem named
as a snapshot conceals it.

### Step 2: Identify the Stocks

What accumulates in this system?
- Trust (between patient and provider,
  between citizen and institution)
- Skills and capabilities
- Resources (financial, social, cognitive)
- Attitudes and beliefs
- Physical infrastructure
- Relationships

For each stock: what flows into it?
What flows out of it? What is the
current level relative to what is needed?

### Step 3: Map the Feedback Loops

**Reinforcing loops keeping the problem in place:**
Ask: "What happens when the problem gets
worse? Does that make the problem even more
likely to get worse?" If yes: reinforcing loop.
Name it. ("Poverty makes education investment
harder, which reduces earning potential,
which deepens poverty.")

**Balancing loops that are too weak:**
Ask: "What should bring this problem back
toward an acceptable state? Why isn't it?"
A balancing loop that should correct but
does not reveals missing or broken feedback.

### Step 4: Locate the Delays

For each reinforcing loop: where is the
delay between action and visible consequence?

Delays longer than the decision-maker's
feedback window produce overshoot. The
decision-maker cannot see the consequence
of their action before they take the next one.

### Step 5: Identify Feedback Gaps

Where is information that exists but is
not flowing to the person who needs it
to make a different decision?

This is the Level 6 scan. Information gaps
are among the most common structural causes
of persistent problems and among the
most tractable to address.

### Step 6: Ask the Goal Question

"What would this system have to be
optimizing for in order to produce the
behavior we observe?"

Compare that to the stated goal.
The gap between actual optimization and
stated goal is the Level 3 problem.

### Step 7: Surface the Paradigm

"What belief about how the world works
makes the current approach seem reasonable?"

This is the Level 2 question. The current
approach always makes sense from within
some paradigm. Naming that paradigm
makes it questionable.

## Stage 2: Leverage Identification and Testing

### The Leverage Scan

For the system mapped in Stage 1,
systematically ask:

**Level 6 (Information flows):**
Who lacks information that would change
their decision? What information exists
but is invisible to them? What new
information flow would be fastest to
create and most likely to change behavior?

**Level 5 (Rules):**
What rule currently prevents the
better outcome? What rule change would
make the current problem impossible
or unlikely? Who controls that rule?
What would change their incentive to change it?

**Level 4 (Self-organization):**
What prevents this system from
adapting toward better outcomes?
What diversity, experimentation, or
feedback capacity is being suppressed?

**Level 3 (Goals):**
What is the system actually optimizing for?
What would it optimize for if the stated
goal were the actual goal? What would
have to change to make that happen?

**Level 2 (Paradigm):**
What belief makes the current approach
seem natural? What alternative belief
would make a different approach natural?
What anomalies does the current paradigm
fail to explain?

### The Leverage Test

For each candidate leverage point:

**Test 1: Mechanism**
If this leverage point changed, what is
the chain of events that produces the
better outcome? Be specific. Not "more
trust leads to better outcomes" but
"if providers receive weekly data on
patient adherence patterns, they will
adjust the dosing conversation at the
3-week mark, which reduces the information
gap that patients experience as abandonment,
which reduces the lapse at week 4."

**Test 2: Failure type**
Which failure type (from the failure taxonomy)
is this intervention most susceptible to?
What does that failure look like specifically
in this context?

**Test 3: Early signals**
What would you see in the first six months
if this intervention is working?
What would you see if it is failing in the
way the most likely failure type predicts?
Can you distinguish between "working slowly"
and "failing"?

**Test 4: Whose leverage**
Who controls this leverage point currently?
Who benefits from keeping it fixed?
Who would be disrupted by changing it?
Who was not asked?

**Test 5: Paradigm test (B-C integration)**
Is this a structural or belief-level leverage point?
If structural: what narrative makes the change legible?
If belief-level: what is the mechanism from
belief change to material outcome change?

## Stage 3: Idea Generation

After Stages 1 and 2, generate three ideas.
Not more. Three.

Why three:
- Fewer than three risks anchoring on the
  first idea that appears plausible
- More than three reduces the rigor of
  specification per idea
- Three forces genuine variation in leverage level:
  one structural, one paradigmatic,
  one that connects both

### For Each Idea, Specify:

**The leverage point:**
Level, name, and specific instantiation.
"Level 6: Information flows. Specifically:
clinicians currently receive adherence data
in aggregate monthly reports. The intervention
routes individual patient adherence data
to the clinician at the point of the next
scheduled appointment."

**The mechanism:**
The causal chain from intervention to outcome.
Stated with the minimum causal steps that
are plausible. Every additional step is
an additional assumption and an additional
failure point.

**The feedback signal:**
What would you see if it is working?
On what timeline?
What is the leading indicator (visible early)
and the lagging indicator (the actual outcome)?

**The failure signature:**
What does failure look like for the most
likely failure type?
What early signals indicate this failure
type is emerging?
What decision would you make if you saw it?

**The ethical question:**
Whose leverage is this?
Who is not in the room?
What does this intervention foreclose?
What power does it redistribute, and to whom?

## The Anti-Patterns to Avoid

**The laundry list:** Generating ten ideas
at Level 12 because it feels productive.
Not ideas: options. The generator produces
hypotheses, not options.

**The inspiring but unspecified:**
"We need to shift the paradigm around X"
without a mechanism from paradigm shift
to material outcome. Definition C without B.

**The measurement-first trap:**
Designing the evaluation before the
leverage hypothesis. Metrics serve the
hypothesis; the hypothesis does not serve
the metrics.

**The consensus idea:**
The idea that everyone in the room
already agrees is good. If it were both
good and uncontested, it would already
have been implemented. Persistent problems
are persistent partly because the easy
ideas have been exhausted.

**The tool-first framing:**
"We should build an app / run a campaign /
create a training program." These are
tools. Tools are not leverage hypotheses.
The question is: what leverage does this
tool apply, through what mechanism,
and how would we know if we were wrong?

---

## Output Quality Markers

A well-specified idea from this generator has:

- A named leverage point at Level 6 or above
- A causal mechanism stated in 2-4 steps
- A feedback signal with a timeline
- A failure signature for the most likely failure type
- An answer to "whose leverage? toward what end?"

An underspecified idea has:
- A vague gesture at a leverage level
  ("we need better information")
- A mechanism stated as correlation
  ("better information leads to better outcomes")
- A success metric borrowed from Definition A
  without connection to the leverage hypothesis
- No failure signature
- No ethical question

If an idea is underspecified after Stage 2,
return to Stage 1. The map is incomplete.
