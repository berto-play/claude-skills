# Paradigm and Narrative: The Shadow Discipline

Definition C is the shadow. It disciplines
Definition B by asking: is this leverage point
structural or is it belief-level? And if it is
belief-level, what is the mechanism through
which belief changes and how does that connect
to material outcomes?

This file is the rigorous version of paradigm-shift
thinking. Not "we need to change the narrative"
as a vague aspiration. The actual mechanics of
how paradigms shift, what accelerates and what
prevents them, and what distinguishes
paradigm-level impact from paradigm-level theater.

---

## The Intellectual Foundation

### Thomas Kuhn: The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962)

The book that gave us "paradigm shift." What Kuhn
actually argued, which is more useful than
the popular version:

**Normal science:** Most scientific work operates
within a paradigm. Scientists solve puzzles
that the paradigm defines as worth solving.
Anomalies that do not fit are set aside,
explained away, or attributed to instrument error.

**Anomaly accumulation:** Over time, anomalies
accumulate that the paradigm cannot accommodate.
The discipline enters a period of crisis.

**Revolution:** A new paradigm emerges that
makes the anomalies coherent. It does not
refute the old paradigm on its own terms:
it offers a different framework within which
the anomalies are no longer anomalies.

**Incommensurability:** The old and new paradigms
are not fully translatable into each other.
Debate between paradigm holders often fails
because they are not answering the same questions.

**What this means for the idea generator:**
- You cannot argue someone out of a paradigm
  by accumulating evidence within the old frame
- Paradigm shifts require showing anomalies
  the old frame cannot explain
- The new frame must make the anomalies coherent,
  not just point to them
- The shift is social as well as cognitive:
  a new paradigm needs a community

Key text: Kuhn: The Structure of Scientific
Revolutions (1962). Read the postscript in
the second edition.

### George Lakoff: Metaphors We Live By (1980)
and Don't Think of an Elephant (2004)

**Conceptual metaphors:** We understand abstract
domains through concrete metaphors. These
metaphors are not just rhetorical: they structure
what we can think and what remains invisible.

**Crime is a disease** → Treatment, prevention,
public health approaches are natural.
**Crime is a moral failure** → Punishment,
responsibility, deterrence are natural.
**Crime is a social product** → Structural
interventions, inequality reduction are natural.

Each metaphor makes certain interventions
natural and others impossible to see.

**Framing:** The way an issue is presented
activates different cognitive frames, which
recruit different values, which produce
different conclusions. Framing is not spin:
it is the structure through which reasoning occurs.

**The design implication:** The idea generator
must ask what metaphor currently structures
the problem. A new leverage hypothesis may
require a new metaphor before it can be heard.

Key texts:
- Lakoff and Johnson: Metaphors We Live By (1980)
- Lakoff: Don't Think of an Elephant (2004)
- Lakoff: Moral Politics (1996) for political framing

### Robert Cialdini: Influence (1984) and Pre-Suasion (2016)

The psychological mechanisms of persuasion.
Not manipulation: mechanisms. Knowing these
is necessary to understand how paradigm-level
ideas actually spread.

Six principles:
1. Reciprocity: people return favors
2. Commitment and consistency: people act
   consistently with prior commitments
3. Social proof: people follow others
4. Authority: people follow credible experts
5. Liking: people are persuaded by people
   they like
6. Scarcity: people value what is rare

Pre-Suasion: attention management. The moment
before a message is delivered determines its
effectiveness. What people are attending to
shapes what they are receptive to.

**For the idea generator:** These mechanisms
operate regardless of the quality of the idea.
A paradigm-level insight that is delivered
without attention to these mechanisms will
not spread. The mechanism of delivery is
part of the intervention.

### Everett Rogers: Diffusion of Innovations (1962)

The empirical study of how ideas spread
through social systems. The S-curve of
adoption: innovators, early adopters, early
majority, late majority, laggards.

**What actually predicts adoption:**
- Relative advantage: is it better than
  the current practice?
- Compatibility: does it fit with existing
  values and practices?
- Complexity: how hard is it to understand
  and use?
- Trialability: can it be tried before
  full adoption?
- Observability: are the results visible
  to others?

**The chasm (Geoffrey Moore, Crossing the Chasm):**
Between early adopters and early majority there
is a gap that most innovations fall into.
Early adopters adopt for their own reasons.
Early majority adopt because they see peers
doing it and it solves a pressing problem.
Different messages and channels are required
for each group.

**For the idea generator:** Paradigm-level
ideas face the same adoption dynamics as
products. The adoption characteristics above
determine the spread strategy, not just
the quality of the idea.

## How Paradigms Actually Change

Drawing on Kuhn, Lakoff, and the sociology
of knowledge:

**Stage 1: Anomaly accumulation**
The current paradigm produces outcomes
it cannot explain on its own terms.
People experience these as isolated problems,
not as symptoms of the paradigm.

**Stage 2: Alternative framework articulation**
Someone articulates a framework within which
the anomalies are coherent. This is rarely
accepted immediately. It is typically dismissed
as naive, too simple, or politically motivated.

**Stage 3: Community formation**
The alternative framework attracts a community
of practitioners who demonstrate its usefulness.
Demonstration is more powerful than argument.

**Stage 4: Institutional entry**
The community gains access to institutions
(journals, funding bodies, training programs)
that legitimize and replicate the framework.

**Stage 5: Old paradigm reframing**
The old paradigm either incorporates elements
of the new one (often distorting both) or
its practitioners retire and the new paradigm
takes over. Rarely is the old paradigm
publicly refuted.

**The design implication:** Paradigm-level
interventions are not communications campaigns.
They require a decade-scale strategy involving
demonstration, community, and institutional entry.
Ideas that claim paradigm-level impact without
this strategy are operating at the level of
provocation, not change.

## Narrative as Mechanism

Narrative is distinct from paradigm. Paradigms
are the deep assumptions that structure what
is thinkable. Narratives are the stories that
carry paradigms in emotionally resonant, memorable,
and transmissible form.

### The Narrative Transportation Theory (Green and Brock)

People who are transported into a narrative
experience reduced counterarguing, increased
identification with characters, and lasting
belief change. Story is not a softer form
of argument: it is a different mechanism
with different effects.

**For the idea generator:** Abstract leverage
hypotheses that operate at the paradigm level
need narrative translation to spread. The idea
itself is not the intervention. The idea plus
its narrative carrier is the intervention.

### The Components of a Transmissible Narrative (Heath and Heath: Made to Stick)

Six principles: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete,
Credible, Emotional, Story (SUCCESs).

- Simple: the core message can be stated in one sentence
- Unexpected: violates expectations enough to create
  attention (but not so much as to create rejection)
- Concrete: expressed in specific, sensory, human terms
- Credible: has an authority or evidence source
- Emotional: people feel something
- Story: has a character, a challenge, and a resolution

**The test for a paradigm-level idea:**
Can this idea be expressed as a story that
a person would retell to a friend?
If not, the idea has not been translated
into a transmissible form yet.

## When Definition C Fails: The Accountability Test

The failure mode of paradigm-level work is
using it to avoid accountability for material outcomes.

**Warning signs:**
- "We're in it for the long game" is used to
  explain why there are no measurable short-term results
- "You can't measure this" is used to prevent
  any evaluation rather than to argue for
  different metrics
- The work produces beautiful articulations
  of the new paradigm that circulate among
  practitioners but never reach the people
  inside the system
- The paradigm-shift claim is made in year 1
  and unchanged in year 5

**The accountability questions for C-level work:**
1. What evidence would convince you that the
   paradigm has not shifted?
2. What is the mechanism between paradigm
   change and material outcome change?
3. Who is the community of practice and
   is it growing or stable?
4. What institutional entry has been achieved?
5. At what point in the above model of
   paradigm change are you currently?

## The B-C Integration

The right way to use Definition C as Definition B's shadow:

**B asks:** What is the leverage point?
**C asks:** Is this leverage point structural or belief-level?

If structural (rules, information flows, incentives):
C contributes by asking what narrative would
make the structural change legible and acceptable
to the people who must change their behavior.

If belief-level (goals, mindsets, paradigms):
C is the primary mechanism, and B contributes
by demanding a model of how belief change
connects to behavioral and material change.

The integration question: "What is the mechanism
through which this paradigm shift produces the
outcome we care about, and what would we see
if the mechanism were working?"
