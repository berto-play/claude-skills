# Leverage Typology: The Twelve Points

Donella Meadows' twelve places to intervene in
a system, ordered from least to most powerful.
This is the operational core of the generator.

Most persistent problems are persistent because
every intervention has operated at levels 12-10.
The generator's job is to identify which higher-
level leverage point has been neglected and why.

---

## Reading This Typology

Each leverage point has:
- What it is
- What intervention at this level looks like
- Why it is more powerful than the levels below it
- The failure mode specific to this level
- Examples from different domains

Counterintuitive principle: The higher the
leverage point, the more resistance you will
encounter when you try to change it. This is
because higher leverage points are more
tightly controlled by those who benefit from
the current system.

---

## Level 12: Numbers (Constants and Parameters)

**What it is:** The numerical values in the
system. Tax rates. Minimum wage. Drug dosage.
Class size. Speed limits.

**Why people go here first:** Numbers are
visible, debatable, and adjustable without
changing the system's structure. Political
systems are organized to debate numbers.

**Why it is the weakest leverage:** Changing
a parameter rarely changes the system's
fundamental behavior. A system structured
to produce poverty will produce poverty
whether the minimum wage is $7.25 or $15.
A system structured to produce recidivism
will produce recidivism whether sentences
are 2 years or 5.

**Failure mode:** Endless parameter adjustment
that never touches the underlying structure.
"Evidence-based policy" that optimizes
parameters within a broken structure.

**When it is the right lever:** When the
problem is genuinely one of miscalibration
within a functional structure. Factory
throughput with a known bottleneck.
Medication dosage in an otherwise effective
treatment. These exist; they are just rarer
than assumed.

## Level 11: Buffer Sizes

**What it is:** The size of stabilizing
stocks relative to the flows into and out
of them. Strategic oil reserves. Hospital
surge capacity. Emergency savings. Inventory.

**Why it matters:** Large buffers absorb
shocks without destabilizing the system.
Small buffers make the system brittle.

**Failure mode:** Building buffers is expensive
and their value is invisible until a shock
arrives. Systems optimized for efficiency
systematically underinvest in buffers.
The result is brittleness that appears
suddenly and catastrophically.

**Examples:**
- Healthcare systems with no surge capacity
  fail during pandemics not because of
  bad treatment protocols but because
  buffer size was optimized away
- Families with no emergency savings
  are one shock away from debt spirals
- Supply chains with just-in-time inventory
  collapse under minor disruptions

## Level 10: Stock-and-Flow Structures

**What it is:** The physical arrangement
of stocks and flows in the system. Pipeline
structures. Bathtub structures. The fact that
you cannot reduce carbon in the atmosphere
faster than the ocean and biosphere absorb it,
regardless of how fast you cut emissions.

**Why it matters:** Some structural features
of systems make certain outcomes physically
impossible to achieve quickly, regardless
of intervention. Knowing the stock-and-flow
structure tells you the fastest possible
rate of change.

**Failure mode:** Interventions that demand
faster change than the structure allows.
Programs that expect transformation in years
in systems that change in decades. Funders
who withdraw when five-year programs show
no effect, not understanding that the
system changes on a fifty-year timescale.

**Design implication:** Structural analysis
before target-setting. If the structure
does not allow the timeline, either change
the structure or change the timeline.

## Level 9: Delays

**What it is:** The time between an action
and its visible consequence. The lag between
carbon emission and temperature change
(decades). Between policy change and
labor market effect (years). Between
educational investment and economic
return (a generation).

**Why delays matter for leverage:**
Delays are the primary cause of oscillation
and overshoot in systems. Actors make
decisions based on current information;
by the time the consequence of past
decisions arrives, the context has changed
and a new overcorrection is underway.

**Intervention options:**
- Reduce the delay (accelerate feedback)
- Make the delay visible so actors can
  reason about it explicitly
- Design interventions that do not require
  fast feedback to succeed

**Failure mode:** Interventions designed
on a feedback timeline shorter than the
system's actual delay. Programs evaluated
before their effects can appear.
Accountability structures that create
perverse incentives because consequences
arrive faster for the wrong behaviors
than for the right ones.

**Examples:**
- Climate policy evaluated on electoral
  cycles when effects appear on decadal cycles
- Teacher effectiveness evaluation on
  annual test scores when learning compounds
  over years
- Drug treatment programs evaluated on
  six-month sobriety when five-year
  outcomes are what matters

## Level 8: Balancing Feedback Loop Strength

**What it is:** The strength of the negative
feedback loops that maintain stability in
the system. How quickly and accurately
a thermostat measures and corrects temperature.
How strongly market prices respond to supply
and demand imbalances.

**Intervention:** Strengthen corrective
feedback loops that are too weak. The system
that should self-correct but does not.

**Failure mode:** Strengthening feedback
loops that are already too strong (creates
oscillation) or weakening ones that are
necessary for stability (creates drift).

**Examples:**
- Corruption persists where the corrective
  feedback loop (prosecution, accountability)
  is too slow or weak
- Organizational dysfunction persists where
  leadership receives filtered information
  (the feedback loop is broken)
- Chronic disease worsens where patients
  cannot feel the consequence of behavior
  change quickly enough

---

## Level 7: Reinforcing Feedback Loop Gain

**What it is:** The strength of the positive
feedback loops that drive growth (or collapse)
in the system. The rate at which success
breeds success, or failure compounds failure.

**Why this is higher leverage than balancing
loops:** Reinforcing loops drive the
system's growth dynamics. Changing their
gain changes the fundamental trajectory.

**Intervention:** Reduce the gain of
reinforcing loops that are driving harmful
growth. Increase the gain of reinforcing
loops that drive beneficial growth.

**Failure mode:** Adding a reinforcing loop
to a system that already has the capacity
you want. Viral mechanics in a product where
the problem is content quality, not
distribution. Incentivizing a behavior
that is already intrinsically motivated.

**Examples:**
- Poverty traps are reinforcing loops:
  low income produces low investment in
  children produces low income in the
  next generation. Breaking any link in
  the loop changes the trajectory.
- Learning compounds: early reading skill
  predicts later academic success. The
  earlier the investment, the larger the
  reinforcing loop gain.
