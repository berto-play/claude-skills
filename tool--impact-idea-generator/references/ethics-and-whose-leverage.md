# Ethics and Whose Leverage

The hard limit. The question this generator
flags and does not resolve.

Every leverage hypothesis encodes a value
judgment: this outcome matters, this change
is better, this is the right direction.
These are not neutral claims. They are ethical
and political positions. Making them explicit
is not a weakness in the analysis: it is
the condition for the analysis being honest.

---

## The Central Questions

Before any idea from this generator is
acted on, three questions must be answered
by the humans who remain in the loop:

**1. Whose leverage?**
Who benefits when this leverage point changes?
Who is disrupted? Who bears the cost of the
transition? Who is not in the room when these
questions are answered?

**2. Toward what end?**
The leverage hypothesis says "this intervention
changes X." What does a changed X produce
for different groups of people? Is that
outcome genuinely better for everyone it affects,
or better for some at the cost of others?

**3. Who decides?**
Who has the authority to implement this
intervention? Who should have that authority?
Are those the same people?

## The Participation Problem

Systems maps are drawn by someone, from somewhere.
Every causal loop diagram reflects the mapmaker's
theory of what causes what. That theory is not
neutral: it reflects the mapmaker's position
in the system.

**The political economy of systems mapping:**
A map drawn by a funder looks different from
a map drawn by a beneficiary.
A map drawn by a policy maker looks different
from a map drawn by the person the policy
is about.
A map drawn by a designer looks different
from a map drawn by a user.

This is not a reason not to map. It is a reason
to ask: who drew this map, what did they see,
and what did they not see?

**The minimum requirement:**
Before proposing a leverage hypothesis, ask:
has anyone affected by this system who is not
in this room been asked to review the map?

## The Escobar Warning

Arturo Escobar and the post-development
critics make a claim that the impact world
has largely not reckoned with:

Development and impact discourse constructs
the "problems" it claims to solve. The act
of naming a condition a "problem" requiring
external intervention encodes a value judgment
about what normal looks like and who has
the authority to define it.

**What this means in practice:**
- "Low educational attainment" as a problem
  assumes that the relevant measure is years
  of formal schooling, that formal schooling
  as currently structured is what is wanted,
  and that the people with low attainment
  would agree they have a problem.
- "Low civic engagement" as a problem assumes
  that the relevant measure is formal political
  participation, that the existing political
  system is worth engaging with, and that
  disengagement reflects a deficit rather than
  a rational response to a system that has
  repeatedly failed.

**The generator's responsibility:**
When a problem is brought to this generator,
ask: who named this a problem? What alternative
framings exist? What would the people most
affected by this condition say the problem is?

## Power Analysis

Every leverage point at Level 5 and above is
currently controlled by someone. Changing it
requires either working with those who control
it or building the power to change it despite
their resistance.

**Power analysis asks:**
- Who currently controls this leverage point?
- What is their interest in keeping it fixed?
- What resources (political, financial, social,
  informational) would be required to change it?
- Who are the potential allies in changing it?
- What is the realistic pathway from current
  state to changed state?

**Without power analysis, leverage hypotheses
are wishes.** "If we just changed the incentive
structure" is a leverage hypothesis. The power
analysis asks: who controls the incentive
structure, what would it take for them to change
it, and what is the theory of how that change
becomes possible?

Key texts:
- Gaventa: "Finding the Spaces for Change"
  (IDS Bulletin, 2006). The power cube framework.
- VeneKlasen and Miller: A New Weave of Power,
  People and Politics. Visible, hidden,
  and invisible power.
- Lukes: Power: A Radical View (1974).
  The three faces of power.

## The Consent Problem at Scale

The generator's scope boundary includes this:
"Problems where the system is actually just a
person: the leverage hypothesis frame becomes
manipulative when applied to individuals
without their consent."

This boundary generalizes:
At what scale does systems intervention
require consent from those in the system?

- A public health intervention that changes
  default organ donation opt-in does not require
  individual consent (and is defensible on
  those grounds)
- A workplace intervention that monitors
  employee behavior to identify "leverage points"
  raises significant consent questions
- A community intervention designed without
  community participation raises the Escobar
  question even if individual consent is obtained

**The working principle:**
The more the intervention targets individuals'
beliefs and behaviors rather than structural
conditions, the stronger the consent requirement.

## What Counts as an Ethical Intervention

There is no consensus answer. The generator
does not provide one. It provides the questions.

**The procedural minimum:**
- People affected by the intervention have
  been consulted about the problem definition
  and the proposed solution
- The power analysis has been conducted
  and the results are disclosed
- The failure signature includes harms,
  not just non-achievement of goals
- There is an exit strategy if harm emerges
- The people who bear the cost of the
  intervention are not different people
  from those who receive its benefits
  (unless this has been explicitly decided
  and disclosed)

**The harder questions:**
- Whose theory of the good is this intervention
  promoting?
- What alternatives were foreclosed when this
  framing was adopted?
- What is the relationship between the interveners
  and the intervened-upon, and does that
  relationship itself need to change?

## Reading the Ethical Question in Output

Every idea generated by this generator
includes an ethical question. That section
is not decorative. It is the minimum
acknowledgment that the answer to "whose
leverage, toward what end" has not been
determined by the system map.

The ethical question in each idea should
be answered before the idea is implemented.

The answer is the responsibility of the
humans who remain in the loop, not the
generator.
