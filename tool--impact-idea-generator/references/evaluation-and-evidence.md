# Evaluation and Evidence

How to know if you are wrong. The discipline
of building in the conditions for your own
falsification before you commit.

This file is the accountability layer.
Without it, the leverage hypothesis is
an untested belief, not a scientific claim.

---

## The Theory of Change

The Theory of Change (ToC) is the most widely
used evaluation framework in impact work.
Its value is not the document: it is the
discipline of making causal assumptions explicit
before intervention begins.

**Core structure:**
Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact

**The systems leverage version:**
Leverage point identified → Intervention designed
→ Mechanism activated → Intermediate signal →
Outcome change → System change

**The critical additions this generator requires:**
- What would we see if the mechanism is wrong?
- What would we see if the leverage point is wrong?
- At what point and based on what evidence
  would we change the hypothesis?

**Common ToC failures:**
- The long chain of assumption: ten causal
  steps with no evidence for any of them
- The missing mechanism: "training leads to
  behavior change" without specifying how
- The unmeasurable outcome: impact defined
  so broadly that no evidence could falsify it
- The absent counterfactual: no account of
  what would have happened without the intervention

Key texts:
- Weiss: Evaluation (1997). The foundational text.
- Pawson and Tilley: Realistic Evaluation (1997).
  The mechanisms-based alternative.
- Rogers: "Theory of Change" (Methodological Briefs,
  UNICEF, 2014). Free online.

## The Counterfactual Problem

The hardest question in impact evaluation:
what would have happened without the intervention?

**Why it matters for systems leverage thinking:**
If the system was already moving toward the
better outcome and the intervention arrived at
the same time, the intervention gets credit
for a change it did not cause.

This is not an abstract concern. It is why:
- Job training programs often show impact
  during economic upturns and disappear
  during recessions
- Health interventions show impact when
  implemented with high-engagement early adopters
  and fail when scaled to the full population
- Educational programs show impact when
  implemented by motivated teachers and
  fail when mandated system-wide

**The minimum viable counterfactual:**
In the absence of a randomized controlled trial,
ask:
1. Is the outcome trending in the direction
   you would predict even before your intervention?
2. Are there comparable contexts without
   your intervention that you can observe?
3. What do the comparable contexts show?

**Difference-in-differences:** Compare change
in your context to change in a comparable
context over the same period.

**Interrupted time series:** Look for a break
in the trend at the point of intervention.

## Leading and Lagging Indicators

The feedback delay problem applied to evaluation.

**Lagging indicators:** The outcomes you
ultimately care about. They move slowly
and are expensive to measure.
(Employment rate, recidivism, health outcomes,
educational attainment, system resilience)

**Leading indicators:** Intermediate outcomes
that are measurable earlier and correlate
with lagging indicators. The early signals
that tell you whether the mechanism is working
before the ultimate outcome is visible.
(Engagement, behavior change, attitude change,
capability demonstration, information flow)

**The danger of leading indicators alone:**
Goodhart's Law. If leading indicators become
targets, they will be optimized without
producing the lagging outcomes.

**The right relationship between leading
and lagging:**
- State the hypothesis about the mechanism
  linking them
- Use leading indicators to test the mechanism
- Use lagging indicators to evaluate the outcome
- If leading indicators improve but lagging
  indicators do not, the mechanism is wrong:
  revise the hypothesis

## Realistic Evaluation (Pawson and Tilley)

The alternative to Theory of Change for
systems-level interventions. Asks not "did
it work?" but "what works, for whom, in
what context, through what mechanism?"

**CMO configuration:**
Context + Mechanism + Outcome

The same intervention produces different
outcomes in different contexts because it
activates different mechanisms. Understanding
the mechanism is what allows you to predict
where an intervention will work and where
it will fail.

**For the idea generator:**
Before proposing an idea, state its CMO:
- Context: what characteristics of the
  situation are necessary for this to work?
- Mechanism: what does the intervention
  activate in the person or system?
- Outcome: what changes as a result?

Then ask: where would the context be different
enough that the mechanism would not activate?
That is where the intervention will fail.

Key text:
- Pawson and Tilley: Realistic Evaluation (1997)
- Pawson: The Science of Evaluation (2013)

## Pre-registration and the Prediction Discipline

The replication crisis in psychology and
social science revealed that many "findings"
were the product of selective analysis:
researchers ran multiple analyses and
reported the significant ones.

**Pre-registration:** Committing to your
hypotheses, analysis plan, and success criteria
before data collection begins. Forces the
distinction between confirmatory analysis
(testing a pre-specified hypothesis) and
exploratory analysis (looking for patterns).

**For impact work:**
Before implementing an intervention, write:
1. The leverage hypothesis
2. The mechanism
3. The leading indicator that will signal
   the mechanism is activating
4. The lagging indicator that will signal
   the outcome is changing
5. The decision rules: if X is not observed
   by Y date, we will revise or stop
6. Who has the authority to call the
   intervention a failure

Register this. Not necessarily in a public
database (though that is best practice) but
at minimum in a document with a date stamp
that cannot be retrospectively altered.

Key resources:
- Open Science Framework (osf.io): free
  preregistration platform
- AsPredicted (aspredicted.org): simpler
  preregistration tool
- PROSPERO: for systematic reviews

## The Decision Rules Requirement

The most underspecified element of most
impact plans is the failure criterion.

**What are the decision rules?**
- If the leading indicator does not show
  X by month 6, we will...
- If the mechanism is not activating
  as hypothesized, we will...
- If the failure type we identified
  begins to emerge, we will...

Without decision rules, evaluations become
exercises in explaining why the intervention
would have worked if circumstances had been different.

**The accountability question:**
Who has the authority to call this a failure?
This person must be named before implementation,
not after.

## Evidence Quality for Different Leverage Levels

Not all impact claims require the same
evidence standard. The standard should match
the leverage level and the stakes.

**Levels 12-9 (parameters, buffers, structures, delays):**
Randomized controlled trials are feasible and
appropriate. The intervention is specific enough
to test. Seek: systematic reviews and RCTs.

**Levels 8-6 (feedback loops, information flows):**
Natural experiments, difference-in-differences,
interrupted time series. The intervention is
larger-scale but the effect may be isolable.

**Levels 5-4 (rules, self-organization):**
Comparative case studies, process tracing,
Ostrom's institutional analysis. The
intervention is systemic; experimental
designs are often not feasible.

**Levels 3-2 (goals, paradigms):**
Qualitative, longitudinal, multi-method.
The mechanism works through meaning-making
and cannot be randomized. Evidence from
sociology of knowledge, communication
research, narrative analysis.

**Level 1 (transcending paradigms):**
Primarily historical and interpretive.
The evidence for paradigm transcendence
is recognized in retrospect.

**The mistake:** Applying Level 12 evidence
standards to Level 2 interventions and
concluding there is no evidence that they work.
