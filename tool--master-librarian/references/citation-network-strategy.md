# Citation Network Strategy

### The Core Idea

Every paper and book exists in a web of citations. 
Citations go in two directions:

**Backward citations (references):**
What this work cites. Shows intellectual ancestry. 
Tells you what to read to understand the foundations. 
Also called ancestor mining or snowball sampling backward.

**Forward citations (citing articles):**
Who cited this work after publication. Shows intellectual 
descendants. Tells you how the field developed, contested, 
or built on this work. Also called forward chaining or 
snowball sampling forward.

A fully trained reader moves in both directions 
from any starting point.

---

### Tools for Citation Navigation

**Connected Papers (connectedpapers.com)**
Visual graph of papers related to a seed paper. 
Shows clusters of related work and temporal 
development. Best for: mapping a new field.

**Semantic Scholar (semanticscholar.org)**
Free. Shows influence metrics, related papers, 
key phrases, and full citation networks. Cleaner 
and more precise than Google Scholar for citation 
analysis.

**Litmaps (litmaps.com)**
Timeline view of a citation network. Shows how 
a field evolved chronologically.

**Google Scholar (scholar.google.com)**
Broadest coverage. "Cited by" link under each 
result shows forward citations. Can be noisy; 
Semantic Scholar is more precise for citation 
counts. Best for: initial discovery.

**Web of Science and Scopus (via library)**
More precise citation data. Better for: 
quantitative analysis of citation counts.

**Citation Gecko (citationgecko.com)**
Upload seed papers, get suggested related papers. 
Best for: building reading lists.

### The Standard Moves

**Move 1: Ancestor mining (backward)**
Start with a key paper or book. Follow its 
references back. Find the foundational texts 
it builds on. Repeat two or three generations 
back to find the origin texts of an idea.

**Move 2: Descendant tracking (forward)**
Use "cited by" in Semantic Scholar or Google 
Scholar to see who built on a work after 
publication. Reveals controversies, replications, 
critiques, and extensions.

**Move 3: Review article mining**
Find a recent review article or Annual Review 
chapter on your topic. These survey the whole 
literature. Use their reference list as a 
pre-curated reading list.

Annual Reviews series (annualreviews.org): 
Annual Review of [Psychology / Sociology / 
Economics / Neuroscience / Political Science / 
etc.]. Many articles free after 1 year.

**Move 4: Author tracking**
Once you find a key researcher, find their full 
publication record. Follow who they cite and 
who cites them. This maps the intellectual 
community of a subfield.

**Move 5: Keyword evolution tracking**
Terms change in a field over time. What was 
called "mental hygiene" is now "mental health." 
What was "race" is now often "ethnicity" or 
"racialization." Knowing how terminology shifted 
is essential for searching historical literature.

### Reading a Literature Systematically

**Phase 1: Orientation (1-3 sources)**
- Find the best textbook or handbook
- Find the most recent Annual Review or similar 
  survey article
- Ask: who are the 5 most cited researchers?

**Phase 2: Canonization (5-15 sources)**
- Identify the 3-5 papers that everyone cites
- Read the foundational books
- Identify the 2-3 major debates

**Phase 3: Current state (10-20 sources)**
- Read the last 2 years of key journals
- Follow preprint servers in the field
- Identify where the frontier is

**Phase 4: Synthesis**
- Map the schools of thought
- Identify unresolved questions
- Note what the consensus is vs. what is 
  genuinely contested

### Evaluating Source Quality

**For journal articles:**
- Is it peer-reviewed?
- Is the journal in DOAJ or indexed in major 
  databases?
- What is the author's h-index? (Google Scholar 
  author profile)
- How many citations has the paper received 
  relative to its age?
- Has it been retracted? (Retraction Watch)
- Is it a preprint? Note this explicitly.

**For books:**
- University press: peer-reviewed manuscript
- Trade press: editorial review but not peer 
  review. Quality varies.
- Self-published: no external review.

**Calibration by field:**
- In physics and mathematics: papers are primary
- In humanities and social science: books often 
  matter more than papers
- In computer science: conference papers matter 
  more than journals
- In medicine: systematic reviews and RCTs 
  over individual studies
- In law: primary sources (cases, statutes) 
  over secondary commentary

### The Anti-Library

Named from Umberto Eco's practice of keeping 
unread books as a record of what you do not know.

A sophisticated researcher maintains awareness of:
- What they have read thoroughly
- What they have read partially or in summary
- What they know exists but have not read
- What they know they do not know exists

The anti-library is the fourth category made 
explicit. When answering a question, knowing 
the shape of your ignorance is as important 
as knowing what you know.

Surface this explicitly when relevant: 
"The literature I am drawing on here is primarily 
Anglophone. There is likely a substantial German 
and French literature on this topic that I am 
not representing."
