# Research Ethics

This file is the operational layer for using knowledge 
responsibly. It completes the stack: what exists, how to 
get it, how to navigate it, and how to use it ethically.

Consult this file whenever the user's work involves 
human subjects, data about people, publication, 
authorship, or research in sensitive domains.

---

## Attribution and Plagiarism

**The core distinction**
Plagiarism is presenting others' work or ideas as your 
own. This applies to text, data, ideas, code, and 
visual material. Norms differ by field but the 
underlying principle does not.

**Citation norms by field**
- Humanities and social sciences: cite sources for 
  all ideas, facts, and quotations. Footnotes or 
  endnotes are common. Chicago and MLA styles dominant.
- Natural sciences: cite sources in-text by number 
  or author-year. Vancouver and APA styles common.
- Law: citation is extremely precise. Bluebook (US) 
  and OSCOLA (UK) are the standard systems. Cases, 
  statutes, and secondary sources each have distinct 
  citation forms.
- Computer science: conference papers use IEEE or ACM 
  style. Code should be cited and licensed.

**Self-plagiarism**
Reusing substantial portions of your own previously 
published work without disclosure is considered 
unethical in most fields. Always disclose prior 
publication.

**Paraphrasing vs. quotation**
Paraphrasing without attribution is still plagiarism. 
The source must be cited regardless of whether you 
quote directly or restate in your own words.

## Authorship

**ICMJE criteria (standard in medicine and sciences)**
The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors 
defines authorship as requiring all four of:
1. Substantial contribution to conception or design, 
   or acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of data
2. Drafting or critically revising the work
3. Final approval of the version to be published
4. Accountability for all aspects of the work

**Ghost authorship**: listing someone who did not 
contribute to the work. Unethical.

**Guest or gift authorship**: listing someone 
(often a senior figure) who did not contribute, 
typically for prestige. Unethical.

**Order of authorship**
- Sciences: first author did the most work, last 
  author is typically the senior/corresponding 
  author. Middle positions are roughly by contribution.
- Humanities: alphabetical order is common, 
  especially in philosophy.
- Always clarify expectations before starting 
  a collaborative project.

**AI authorship**
As of 2024-2025, major journals and publishers 
require disclosure of AI tool use in manuscript 
preparation. AI systems cannot be listed as authors 
under ICMJE criteria because they cannot be held 
accountable. Check the specific journal policy; 
these are evolving rapidly.

## Human Subjects Research

**Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) / Ethics Committees**
Any research involving human participants typically 
requires IRB or ethics committee approval before 
data collection begins. This applies to:
- Surveys and questionnaires
- Interviews
- Behavioral experiments
- Secondary analysis of identifiable data
- Some observational research

Exemptions exist (e.g., publicly available data, 
certain educational research) but must be formally 
determined by the IRB, not assumed by the researcher.

**Informed consent**
Participants must understand what the research 
involves, what risks it poses, how their data 
will be used, and that participation is voluntary. 
Consent must be documented.

**Special populations**
Additional protections apply to children, prisoners, 
pregnant women, people with cognitive impairments, 
and others considered vulnerable. Requirements vary 
by jurisdiction.

**Deception in research**
Some social psychology research involves deception. 
This requires IRB approval, a strong scientific 
justification that the research cannot be conducted 
otherwise, and a debriefing protocol.

**Key frameworks**
- Belmont Report (1979): the foundational US document 
  on human subjects research ethics. Three principles: 
  respect for persons, beneficence, justice.
- Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association): 
  international standard for medical research.
- GDPR (EU General Data Protection Regulation): 
  governs data collection, processing, and storage 
  for EU residents. Applies regardless of where the 
  researcher is based if data involves EU residents.
- HIPAA (US): governs health information. Applies to 
  covered entities and their business associates.

## Data Ethics and Privacy

**Anonymization vs. pseudonymization**
Anonymization removes all identifying information 
such that re-identification is not possible. 
Pseudonymization replaces identifiers with codes, 
but re-identification may be possible with additional 
data. GDPR treats these differently.

**Data minimization**
Collect only what you need for the stated purpose. 
This is both an ethical principle and a legal 
requirement under GDPR.

**Sensitive data categories**
Require heightened protection: race and ethnicity, 
political opinions, religious beliefs, trade union 
membership, genetic data, biometric data, health 
data, sexual orientation.

**Data sharing and open data**
Increasingly required by funders and journals. 
Must be balanced against participant privacy. 
Solutions include: aggregated data only, 
access-controlled repositories, synthetic data, 
and data use agreements.

**Secondary data use**
Using datasets collected for one purpose for 
another purpose may raise ethical issues even 
if legally permissible. Consider whether 
participants would have consented to this use.

## Indigenous Knowledge and Research

Research involving Indigenous communities, 
knowledge, or cultural heritage requires 
special ethical attention beyond standard IRB review.

**Key principles (OCAP framework: Canada)**
- Ownership: community owns its cultural knowledge 
  and data
- Control: community controls research processes
- Access: community has access to data about itself
- Possession: community maintains physical control 
  of data

**CARE principles for Indigenous data governance**
- Collective benefit
- Authority to control
- Responsibility
- Ethics

**Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC)**
Consulting with and obtaining consent from 
Indigenous communities before research that 
affects them. Distinguished from individual 
informed consent.

**Practical guidance**
- Involve community members as co-researchers 
  where possible
- Return research findings to the community
- Ensure community benefits from the research
- Consult: AIATSIS Code of Ethics (Australia), 
  Tri-Council Policy Statement Chapter 9 (Canada), 
  UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples

## Conflicts of Interest

**Disclosure requirements**
Most journals and funding agencies require disclosure 
of financial and other interests that could influence 
research. This includes:
- Funding sources (who paid for the research)
- Employment by entities with a stake in the findings
- Personal financial interests (stock ownership, 
  consulting fees, patents)
- Personal relationships with editors or reviewers

**Why this matters**
Studies funded by industry consistently show more 
favorable results for sponsors' products than 
independent studies of the same interventions. 
Disclosure allows readers to weigh this.

**Non-financial conflicts**
Ideological commitments, personal relationships, 
and career incentives can also bias research. 
These are harder to disclose but worth naming 
where they may affect interpretation.

## Publication Ethics

**Duplicate publication**
Submitting the same manuscript to two journals 
simultaneously is prohibited. Publishing the 
same findings twice without disclosure is 
considered unethical.

**Selective reporting**
Reporting only significant results while 
suppressing null findings distorts the 
literature. Pre-registration (at OSF, ClinicalTrials.gov, 
or AsPredicted) addresses this by committing to 
hypotheses and analyses before data collection.

**Data fabrication and falsification**
The most serious forms of research misconduct. 
Can result in retraction, career consequences, 
and in some fields legal liability.

**Peer review ethics**
Reviewers should not use access to unpublished 
manuscripts for personal advantage. Conflicts 
of interest with authors should be disclosed. 
Review should be constructive, not obstructive.

**Retraction**
When serious errors or misconduct are identified, 
papers should be retracted. Authors, editors, and 
institutions all have responsibilities here. 
Retraction Watch tracks these.
